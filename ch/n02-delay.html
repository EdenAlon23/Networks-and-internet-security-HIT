Delay, loss and throughput

Lecture 2 turns the picture of packets moving through routers into numbers. Almost every exam has a calculation built from the four delays below, so this chapter gives each formula, explains where it comes from, and solves the classic question types step by step.

1The four sources of packet delay

At each router (node) on the path, a packet suffers four delays. Their sum is the nodal delay:

d_nodal = d_proc + d_queue + d_trans + d_prop
DelayWhat causes itFormula / sizeDepends on
Processing dprocCheck bit errors, read the header, look up the output linkmicroseconds or lessRouter hardware
Queueing dqueueWait in the output buffer until earlier packets are transmitted0 to ms; grows with traffic intensityCongestion level
Transmission dtransPush all L bits of the packet onto the linkL / RPacket length L, link rate R
Propagation dpropOne bit travels the physical length of the linkd / sLink length d, propagation speed s ≈ 2·108 m/s
Exam trap

Transmission and propagation are completely unrelated: L/R does not depend on distance, and d/s does not depend on packet size or link rate. A question that gives you a 1 Gbps link and asks for propagation delay wants only d/s.

End to end over N links (N−1 routers), with no queueing and identical links: dend-end = N · (dproc + dtrans + dprop). With different links, sum each link's own L/Ri + di/s.

2Transmission vs propagation: the caravan analogy

Cars = bits, a caravan of cars = a packet, a toll booth = a router transmitting onto a link, the highway = the link.

The 10-car caravan

Given

  • 10 cars, a toll booth serves one car every 12 s (the "transmission rate")
  • Cars drive at 100 km/h (the "propagation speed"); the next booth is 100 km away

Solution

  1. Time to push the whole caravan through the booth: 10 × 12 s = 120 s (= L/R)
  2. Time for the last car to drive to the next booth: 100 km / (100 km/h) = 1 h (= d/s)
  3. The whole caravan lines up at the next booth after 62 minutes

Now make the cars drive at 1,000 km/h and the booth take 1 minute per car. Do cars reach the second booth before all cars pass the first? Yes: the first car arrives after 7 minutes (1 min service + 6 min drive) while 3 cars are still at the first booth. In network terms: the first bit of a packet can reach the next router before the last bit has left the previous one when dprop < dtrans. That is normal for long packets on short, slow links.

Where is the first bit? (classic exam question)

Given

  • Hosts A and B are m meters apart, link rate R bps, speed s m/s, packet L bits. A starts transmitting at t = 0.

Solution

  1. At t = dtrans = L/R the last bit has just left A.
  2. If dprop > dtrans, the first bit is still on the link, at distance s · L/R from A.
  3. If dprop < dtrans, the first bit has already arrived at B.
  4. They are equal when m = s · L / R. Example: s = 2.5·108, L = 1,500 B = 12,000 bit, R = 10 Mbps → m = 2.5·108 × 1.2·10-3 = 300 km.

3Queueing delay, traffic intensity and loss

Let a be the average packet arrival rate (packets/s) at an output link of rate R, all packets L bits. The traffic intensity is the ratio of the bit arrival rate to the service rate:

ρ = L · a / R
ρAverage queueing delayWhy
ρ ≈ 0SmallPackets rarely find others ahead of them
ρ → 1Grows very fast (non-linearly)Bursts arrive faster than the link drains them, and the queue never fully empties
ρ > 1Infinite in theory; in practice the buffer fills and packets are droppedMore work arrives on average than can be served
Remember

Design rule: never design a system with ρ > 1. Even ρ = 0.9 already means large delays, because traffic is bursty, not evenly spaced.

A burst of N packets arriving at once to an empty queue: the n-th packet (n = 1..N) waits (n − 1) · L/R, so the average queueing delay of the burst is (N − 1) · L / (2R).

Packet loss. A queue (buffer) has finite capacity. A packet that arrives to a full queue is dropped. A lost packet may be retransmitted by the previous node, by the source (TCP), or not at all (UDP).

4Bits in flight: the bandwidth-delay product

How many bits are "on the wire" at once? Multiply the rate by the propagation delay: R · dprop. This is the maximum number of bits that can be in the link, and it reappears in TCP: a sender needs a window of at least R · RTT to keep a long fat pipe full (chapter N07).

Two hosts 20,000 km apart

Given

  • R = 2 Mbps, s = 2.5·108 m/s, d = 2·107 m

Solution

  1. dprop = 2·107 / 2.5·108 = 0.08 s
  2. R · dprop = 2·106 × 0.08 = 160,000 bits in the link at most
  3. The "width" of one bit on the link: s / R = 2.5·108 / 2·106 = 125 m
  4. An 800,000-bit file sent as one message: 800,000 / 2·106 + 0.08 = 0.48 s

5Throughput and the bottleneck link

Throughput is the rate (bits/s) at which bits actually arrive at the receiver. Instantaneous throughput is the rate at a given moment; average throughput is F / T for a file of F bits that took T seconds.

A path is only as fast as its slowest link, the bottleneck: with a server link Rs and a client link Rc, throughput = min(Rs, Rc). The file transfer time is then roughly F / min(Rs, Rc) (ignoring propagation and queues).

10 connections sharing a core link

Given

  • 10 servers and 10 clients; each server link Rs = 2 Mbps, each client link Rc = 1 Mbps; all 10 connections cross one core link of R = 5 Mbps, shared equally

Solution

  1. Each connection gets at most R / 10 = 0.5 Mbps on the shared link
  2. Throughput per connection = min(Rs, Rc, R/10) = min(2, 1, 0.5) = 0.5 Mbps
  3. If the core link were 50 Mbps: min(2, 1, 5) = 1 Mbps, and the client access link becomes the bottleneck. In today's Internet the access links are usually the bottleneck, because the core is over-provisioned.

6Why break messages into packets: message segmentation

Message switching vs packet switching

Given

  • Message of 8·106 bits, source → 2 routers → destination (3 links), every link 2 Mbps, no propagation/queueing

Solution

  1. Without segmentation: one hop takes 8·106 / 2·106 = 4 s; store-and-forward over 3 hops = 12 s
  2. With 800 packets of 10,000 bits: one packet per hop = 104 / 2·106 = 5 ms
  3. First packet arrives after 3 × 5 = 15 ms; then one more every 5 ms: (3 + 800 − 1) × 5 ms = 4.01 s
  4. Segmentation pipelines the links, so it is about 3× faster here. It also means a bit error costs one small packet, not the whole message. Downsides: header overhead per packet, and reassembly at the receiver.

7Measuring real delays: traceroute

traceroute (Windows: tracert) measures the delay from the source to every router on the path. For each hop i it sends 3 probes with TTL = i. The i-th router decrements TTL to 0, drops the probe and returns an ICMP Time Exceeded (type 11) message, revealing its address. The source measures the round trip for each probe. It stops when the destination answers (ICMP Port Unreachable for UDP probes, or Echo Reply for ICMP probes).

$ traceroute gaia.cs.umass.edu
 1  cs-gw (128.119.240.254)            1 ms   1 ms   2 ms
 2  border1-rt-fa5-1-0.gw.umass.edu   1 ms   1 ms   2 ms
 ...
 8  62.40.103.253 (de2-1.de1.de.geant.net)  104 ms 109 ms 106 ms   ← trans-oceanic link
 9  de2-1.de1.de.geant.net            109 ms 102 ms 104 ms
 ...
17  * * *                                                         ← no reply (filtered / lost)
19  fantasia.eurecom.fr                132 ms 128 ms 136 ms
  • A sudden jump between two hops (here 8) usually means a long link: propagation across an ocean.
  • Sometimes hop n+1 shows a smaller delay than hop n: each probe is a separate measurement with its own queueing delay, so it varies.
  • * * * means no reply within the timeout: the router does not send ICMP, or a firewall filters it.
  • Each line is a round trip (RTT), so it includes the return path, which may be different.

9Review

Which delay depends on distance, and which on packet size?

Propagation d/s depends on distance; transmission L/R on packet size and link rate.

Traffic intensity is 0.95. What do you expect?

Large and growing average queueing delay, and loss if buffers are small.

Throughput of a path with links of 10, 2 and 100 Mbps?

2 Mbps: the bottleneck link sets the rate.

Why does traceroute send packets with increasing TTL?

So that router i drops the probe with TTL = i and replies with ICMP Time Exceeded, revealing itself and the RTT to it.

How many bits fit on a 1 Gbps link with 10 ms propagation?

R · dprop = 109 × 0.01 = 107 bits.
