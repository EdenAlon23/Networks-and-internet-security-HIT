1What cyber security is, and why it is hard to define

NIST: preventing damage to, protecting and restoring computers, communication systems and services, and the information in them, to ensure availability, integrity, authentication, confidentiality and non-repudiation. Shorter: protecting information by preventing, detecting and responding to attacks.

The practical goal has two sides: allow the intended use of a system, and prevent unintended use that could cause harm. What do we protect? Files and personal data, money in the bank, privacy, location privacy.

Why you cannot measure it

There is no "security meter". You cannot say in absolute terms which OS is more secure, whether more money means more security, or whether open source is safer. Security is a property of the whole system against a specific adversary, not a number.

Lecture example: a backdoor attempt in the Linux kernel (2003)

if ((options == (__WCLONE|__WALL)) && (current->uid = 0))
        retval = -EINVAL;

Look at = instead of ==. It is an assignment, not a comparison: a process that calls wait4() with that rare flag combination gets uid 0, i.e. root. The expression itself evaluates to 0 (false), so retval does not change and nobody notices. It looks like a typo; it is a full privilege escalation.

Exploit

A local attacker makes one system call with "odd" flags and becomes root. A backdoor disguised as a bug is very hard to catch in code review.

Real world

It was caught because the change appeared in the CVS mirror without a legitimate source in BitKeeper. The same idea returned in 2024 with xz-utils (CVE-2024-3094): a "trusted" maintainer built trust for two years and slipped a backdoor into sshd through a compression library. (The xz example is my addition, not from the slides.)

Why it matters

Every attack in this course breaks at least one property. When analysing an attack, first ask "which property broke?". Sniffing breaks C, ARP poisoning breaks I (and often C), a SYN flood breaks A. At packet level: C fails when payload is readable on the wire, I fails when a field can be changed without detection, A fails when some finite resource (bandwidth, CPU, a state table) is exhausted.

3The operational model: Protection = Prevention + (Detection + Response)

Perfect prevention does not exist (zero-days, human error). The model assumes breach: the time from intrusion to detection (dwell time) and from detection to response decides how big the damage gets.

Real world

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework extends this to Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover. In companies: firewall and EDR for prevention, a SOC with a SIEM for detection, an IR team for response. Detection at packet level means IDS signatures (Snort, Suricata) and flow logs (NetFlow).

4MOM: what an attacker needs to succeed

Method

Knowledge, skills and tools to carry out the attack.
Defense: hardening, patching, shrinking the attack surface.

Opportunity

Time and access: an open port, an employee who clicks a link, open Wi-Fi.
Defense: least privilege, segmentation, monitoring.

Motive

Money, politics, espionage, curiosity.
Defense: deterrence, enforcement, making the attack unprofitable.

Remove any one of the three and the attack fails. Defenders barely control motive, so most defenses target method and opportunity. Every attack needs a goal and a method.

5Security approaches, defense in depth and diversity

Ignore security

Minimum security, out-of-the-box settings, default passwords, open services. Exactly what botnets like Mirai feed on.

Host security

Each machine protects itself (AV, host firewall, patches). Useful, but does not scale alone; one forgotten machine breaks everything.

Network security

A global view, protecting every point in the network. Hard to manage because no two networks are alike.

Layered security (defense in depth): a bank

Why not check everything in one layer

Performance. An edge firewall sees millions of packets; deep inspection of each would slow the network. So work is split: coarse filtering outside (headers: IPs, ports, flags), deeper inspection (payload, users) closer to the asset.

The attacker's path to the database (lecture)
  1. Get through the outer firewall.
  2. Use packets and techniques the IDS does not recognize.
  3. Get past an inner router doing packet filtering.
  4. Get through an inner firewall separating internal networks.
  5. Break the DB access control (dictionary / brute force).
  6. Find the data inside the DB.
  7. Bypass the ACLs that decide who reads or changes each record.

Diversity of defence

If all layers are identical, breaking one breaks them all. So use different filtering (firewall 1 blocks FTP, SNMP, Telnet; firewall 2 blocks SSL, SSH) and different vendors (Check Point first, Cisco PIX second), so a flaw in one product is not in the other.

Exploit

Attackers look for the uniform link: if every server runs the same version, one exploit works on all of them (WannaCry and NotPetya spread over SMB this way). Lateral movement abuses a flat network with no internal layers.

Real world

A DMZ between two firewalls, VLAN segmentation, a WAF in front of web apps, EDR on endpoints, MFA on access. Zero Trust is the modern continuation: do not trust what is already inside either.

6Least privilege and access control

Every user or program gets exactly the permissions its role needs and no more. A user accesses their own files and selected server files, and that is it.

Lecture example: the accounting department creates an Accounting group, adds the employees, and grants permissions to the group rather than to people. That is RBAC (role-based access control). The principles are implemented as an access control model.

Authentication vs access control

Authentication = proving who you are. Access control (authorization) = what you may access. Proving to the bank that you are you does not give you access to every account.

Exploit

When the principle is ignored: a service running as root/SYSTEM means compromise = full control. An admin who falls for phishing hands over the whole domain. Privilege escalation and IDOR (accessing another user's object because authorization was never checked) attack access control itself.

Real world

Linux rwx permissions, Active Directory groups, AWS IAM policies, sudo instead of logging in as root, containers running as non-root.

7The three authentication factors

Something you know

Password, PIN.
Attacks: brute force, dictionary, phishing, credential stuffing.

Something you have

Smart card, phone with OTP, FIDO key.
Attacks: theft, SIM swapping.

Something you are

Biometrics: fingerprint, face.
Problem: you cannot "replace" a leaked finger.

Authentication has two steps: identify ("who are you": user ID) and prove (password). Combining two factors of different types is MFA. Two passwords are still one factor.

8Building an authentication protocol: ap1.0 to ap5.0

Goal: Bob wants Alice to prove her identity over a network where he cannot "see" her. Trudy is the attacker. Each version fixes the previous weakness and breaks in a new way. Notice how each break is really about a field in a packet.

Where the real problem is

The RSA math is fine. The problem is that Bob asked for the public key over the same channel the attacker controls. Without a way to verify that K⁺ really belongs to Alice, the protocol falls.

The fix

A Certificate Authority (PKI): a trusted third party signs the statement "this key belongs to Alice". That is exactly what HTTPS does: the browser checks that the server certificate is signed by a CA it trusts (chapter 07).

Exploits in the real world
  • Replay: car key fobs (RollJam), replaying cookies or tokens without short expiry, KRACK on WPA2 (replaying message 3 of the handshake forces nonce reuse).
  • IP spoofing: the basis of reflection/amplification DDoS and SYN floods (chapter 05).
  • MITM: ARP poisoning and DNS spoofing on a LAN (chapter 04), evil-twin Wi-Fi, SSL stripping, fake certificates (DigiNotar 2011).
Where you see challenge-response

Kerberos and NTLM in Windows, the WPA2 4-way handshake (ANonce/SNonce), SSH (the server key is checked against known_hosts: trust on first use instead of a CA), TLS (client/server random are nonces).

9The price of security, and why it is so hard

Security is never free: development cost, performance overhead, user inconvenience. Nobody wants to pay more for security than they stand to lose, so the goal is risk analysis, not perfect security: risk ≈ probability × damage, and invest accordingly.

An active, malicious adversary

Turns bugs into exploits, adapts to defenses, waits for mistakes, attacks people when systems are secure. The defender must close everything; the attacker needs one hole.

The supply chain

A phone app depends on developers, SDKs, open source, the OS, the CPU and the carrier. Every link is a vector: SolarWinds 2020, xz 2024.

Solutions exist, nobody uses them

People install Flash "because the site asked"; sites used SSL only on the login page. Better now (HTTPS everywhere, HSTS), but the pattern repeats.

It is a process problem

Security touches all of IT and HR. Example: Microsoft SDL builds security into every development stage (threat modeling, code review, fuzzing).

Economics

No incentive for secure software; security is expensive and slow. Time-to-market wins.

People

Engineers without security training and unaware users. Example: the "Nigerian prince" advance-fee scam, social engineering that has worked for decades.

Defense against off-path attackers

A random identifier ("cookie") that must come back in the reply. In DNS that is the 16-bit Transaction ID plus a random source port. An off-path attacker cannot see them and must guess; a reply with the wrong value is dropped. TCP uses the same idea with a random initial sequence number.

Real world

16 bits = 65,536 options, which was not enough: Kaminsky (2008) showed you can retry endlessly with random subdomains and poison the cache. The fix: random source ports (about 216 × 216 ≈ 4 billion combinations), and long-term DNSSEC, which signs the answers.

11Who the attackers are

Hackers

Motive: politics, intelligence (states), cyber espionage.

Crackers

Motive: money. Organized crime, ransomware.

Script kiddies

Small money and curiosity. Use ready-made tools without understanding them in depth.

The modern profile is not "a young computer genius": ages 12 to 60, any gender, unknown background, all skill levels, insiders or outsiders.

State actors

  • State actors: states conducting cyber operations themselves (Russia, China, USA, Israel, Iran and others).
  • State-sponsored: non-state groups a state runs or funds (proxies), giving the state deniability.
  • Non-state / hacktivists: cybercrime or "patriotic hacking", e.g. Anonymous.
Lecture example

Estonia 2007: after a dispute over moving a Soviet soldier statue, about three weeks of DDoS paralysed banks, government and media. Attributed to Russia. One of the first times a whole country was attacked in cyberspace; NATO's CCDCOE in Tallinn was founded afterwards.

Why the threat keeps growing

Mobile, social networks, embedded systems (IoT), exponential growth in incidents, complex software and targeted attacks, uniform technology (one flaw hits millions), and constant scanning and mapping of networks (chapter 03).

12Anonymity, TOR and the black market

Onion routing: how TOR works

Why three relays

No single relay knows both the source and the destination. To unmask Alice you need to control both the guard and the exit of the same circuit (or watch both edges of the network). At packet level: each cell is 512 bytes, encrypted in three layers; each relay removes one layer and only learns the next hop.

Weaknesses and attacks
  • Malicious exit node: sees cleartext if the site is not HTTPS; can sniff passwords or do SSL stripping.
  • Traffic correlation: an adversary watching traffic enter and leave matches timing and sizes.
  • Attacking the endpoint: a browser exploit makes the machine contact a server directly and reveal its real IP. The FBI used such a NIT in Operation Playpen (2015).
  • User OPSEC: Ross Ulbricht (Silk Road, 2013) was caught through an old post with his personal email, not by breaking TOR.

The lecture says TOR "cannot be traced". In practice it gives strong anonymity against most adversaries, but not against a global adversary or a careless user.

Deep web vs darknet

Deep web = everything not indexed (mail, banking, internal systems). Darknet = networks that need special software such as TOR. According to the lecture, criminal traffic runs there: mail, instant messaging and social networks, as well as terror and activist groups. The Hidden Wiki indexes onion sites, including sales of 0-day exploits.

Anonymous payment

Credit cards and PayPal only work if stolen; cash is impractical. Hence Bitcoin and Liberty Reserve (Costa Rica, shut down by the US in 2013). Bitcoin is pseudonymous, not anonymous: every transaction is on a public blockchain and authorities trace it (Chainalysis), so criminals moved to Monero and mixers.

How authorities respond

  • Spreading malware to track darknet traffic (e.g. Anonymous distributing an infected TOR app to locate paedophiles).
  • Decoys for espionage: a malicious document the target downloads and opens.
  • Intelligence before the event instead of investigation after the crime: looking for anomalies in available data (links to OSINT, chapter 02).
  • Undercover agents who join the community and catch targets in the act.
