OSINT and reconnaissance

Every attack starts with reconnaissance. What an attacker looks for about an organization and its people, the three levels of recon (passive, semi-passive, active), the tools for each and the packets they produce, and the social engineering that turns information into access.

1Reconnaissance: why attackers start here

The Oxford dictionary defines reconnaissance as gathering information about an area for military purposes. A cyber attack is no different: the attacker spends a lot of time observing and probing the target network, because they need a good estimate of the enemy's strengths and weaknesses before acting.

OSINT (Open Source INTelligence) is finding, selecting and collecting information from publicly available sources and analysing it to produce useful military, political or business intelligence.

Why it works

Each piece alone looks harmless: a job ad, a photo from the office, a DNS record. Together they map the organization: which technologies, who works there, who has access, what a legitimate email looks like. The better the recon, the shorter and quieter the attack.

Real world

In the Lockheed Martin Cyber Kill Chain and in MITRE ATT&CK, reconnaissance is the first stage. Red teams and pentesters start every engagement with OSINT; threat-intelligence teams do the same on their own organization to see what an attacker would see.

2Three levels of reconnaissance

The more active you get, the more accurate and current the information, but you pay in exposure. A sophisticated attacker exhausts passive sources first, and only then touches the target, focused and slowly.

3What to look for about an organization

Corporate OSINT · physical

Locations, footprint (how many branches), competitors.

Corporate OSINT · logical

Product lines, job openings, meetings, donations and relationships.

Exploit

Job ads are a gold mine: "network admin with Cisco ASA, VMware and SAP experience" tells you exactly what is installed. The org chart tells you whom to impersonate (the CEO) and whom to target (secretary, finance). The email format (first.last@) lets you generate a phishing target list.

Maltego

A visual tool: give it an entity (domain, person, email) and run transforms that find related entities: subdomains, IPs, emails, profiles. The result is a relationship graph of the organization. Lecture example: a Russian social network site that "volunteered" which software it runs.

Real world

Tools: Pipl and PeopleFinder (people search), theHarvester (emails and subdomains), Sherlock (a username across sites), ExifTool (metadata in photos and documents). Covert gathering can be onsite (e.g. scanning Wi-Fi from a car near the building) or offsite (Google Earth, Street View).

Defense

A publishing policy for employees, stripping metadata from public documents, awareness training, non-guessable email formats, and monitoring what the organization exposes (attack surface management).

5Stuxnet as an OSINT example

Stuxnet was a worm that attacked the centrifuges at Iran's Natanz enrichment plant (discovered 2010). It changed rotor speeds through Siemens PLCs while showing operators "normal" values.

The lecture shows public photos from the Iranian president's visit to the plant: the screens in the background show the SCADA interface with the centrifuge cascade layout, and another photo shows control components. Researcher Ralph Langner used exactly such photos to confirm that the worm targeted Natanz's specific configuration (the number of centrifuges per cascade).

The lesson

Even a top-secret facility leaks technical detail through PR photos. Whoever built the attack needed the exact configuration, and whoever investigated it could verify that from open sources.

6Passive recon tools

WHOIS (RFC 3912)

Who registered the domain: name, address, phone, technical and admin contact emails, DNS servers, dates. Example: hit.ac.il shows a contact name and phone.
On the wire: one TCP connection to port 43 of the registry, the domain name as text, the record back as text.
Exploit: real names and emails for phishing, a phone number for pretexting. Today most registrars hide details (privacy, GDPR).

nslookup / dig

DNS queries: A (domain → IP), MX (mail servers), NS (authoritative servers), SOA (authority info), PTR / reverse (IP → name).
On the wire: UDP 53 to your resolver; with dig @ns1.target straight to the target's server (semi-passive).
Exploit: MX reveals the mail provider (Office 365?), reverse DNS reveals internal server names.

Google dorking

site:hit.ac.il lists every indexed subdomain; also filetype:pdf, intitle:"index of", "@hit.ac.il".
Exploit: documents that should not be public, forgotten login pages, config files.

Subdomain enumeration

Knock and similar scripts try a wordlist (mail, vpn, dev, test) as DNS queries. Today also crt.sh (Certificate Transparency logs list every certificate issued).
Exploit: dev. and test. are less protected than the main site.

Employees (LinkedIn, company site)

Who works where in which role. Example: searching "Holon Institute of Technology" on LinkedIn.
Exploit: a phishing target list and names to impersonate.

Shodan

A "search engine for devices": it scans the whole Internet and stores banners. Finds cameras, routers, SCADA systems and servers by version and location.
Exploit: port:3389 country:IL or a specific vulnerable version, without sending one packet to the target.

Why Shodan is still "passive"

Shodan already scanned the target. The attacker only queries Shodan's database, so the target sees no traffic from the attacker. The same principle makes all the tools in this section undetectable.

Semi-passive: browsing the organization's site like any user. Lecture example: the HIT site itself lists staff names, departments and services. The target only sees "another visitor" in its logs.

7Active reconnaissance

Port scanning with nmap: send probes to ports and see what answers. For each port the result is open, closed or filtered, plus the service name (http, ssh, smtp). Chapter 03 goes packet by packet.

Banner grabbing with netcat

$ nc 192.168.100.194 80
HEAD / HTTP/1.1

HTTP/1.1 400 Bad Request
Content-Type: text/html; charset=us-ascii
Server: Microsoft-HTTPAPI/2.0      ← OS and version revealed
Date: Fri, 01 Mar 2013 12:49:07 GMT
Connection: close

The lecture's technique: send a malformed request and read the error, which leaks information. This request lacks a Host header, so the server returns 400, and its Server header says what it is. On the wire: a full TCP handshake, one PSH/ACK with the request, one with the response, FIN.

Exploit

Exact version → search Exploit-DB or CVE → ready-made exploit. That is how recon becomes an attack.

Defense

Hide or fake banners (ServerTokens Prod in Apache, server_tokens off in nginx), generic error pages, an IDS that detects scans, and closing every port that does not need to be open.

8Social engineering

"All warfare is based on deception... Hold out baits to entice the enemy. Feign disorder, and crush him." Sun Tzu, The Art of War

Social engineering attacks the person instead of the computer, using OSINT to be believable.

Pretexting

Pretending to be someone you are not, to get something you probably should not have, and use it in a way that is probably wrong. Needs prior research (accounts, emails, IDs).
Targets: secretaries, helpdesk, new employees. Building trust: real names, internal jargon, urgency, authority.

Phishing

An email or message impersonating a trusted party that leads to a malicious link or file. Spear phishing = aimed at one person with OSINT detail. Whaling = aimed at executives.

Exit strategy

Every plan needs a way out if it fails, so the attempt is not exposed and the target is not "burned".

Why it works

Cialdini's principles of influence: authority ("I'm from IT"), urgency ("your account locks in an hour"), liking and trust, reciprocity. People want to help, and that is the weakness.

Real world

The 2020 Twitter breach (phone pretexting of employees, takeover of Obama's and Musk's accounts), MGM Resorts 2023 (a helpdesk call to reset MFA, about $100M damage), and business email compromise leading to fraudulent transfers. Testing tools: GoPhish, SET (Social-Engineer Toolkit).

9Attack tree: getting the password from the secretary

Defense

A clear policy: IT never asks for a password. Call back on a known number, phishing drills and training, and phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO2) so a stolen password alone is not enough.
